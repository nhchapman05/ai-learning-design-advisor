# The Three Friction Types Framework
**A Strengthening Tool for Learning Design in Any Environment**
*Natasha Chapman, PhD | Lit Learning LLC*

---

## The Core Idea

This framework meets you wherever you are. You might be skeptical of AI in learning and looking for a principled way to think it through. You might be ready to incorporate AI but want to do it grounded in evidence rather than hype. You might simply not know whether AI belongs in a particular learning experience -- or where, or how much. All of those are reasonable starting points, and this framework works from all of them.

The underlying idea is straightforward. Friction, as used here, means the effort a learner or system expends that is not automatically productive. Not all of that friction is the same. Some friction slows learners down without building anything. Some friction makes complex ideas accessible. Some friction is the learning itself -- the productive struggle that builds real capability. Confusing these leads to designs that eliminate the wrong things, automate the wrong things, or add tools where none were needed.

This framework distinguishes three types of friction present in any learning experience or training environment -- workplace, higher education, community-based, or otherwise. Each type calls for a different design response. The goal is not to eliminate friction. It is to put the right kind of friction in the right place, and to make more intentional decisions about where -- and whether -- AI belongs.

---

## Type 1: Administrative Friction

**Definition:** The overhead that sits between a learner and the learning. Scheduling, enrollment, reminders, reporting, formatting, tracking, logistics, compliance documentation. None of it builds capability. All of it consumes attention and time.

Administrative friction is the friction that should be automated. When a learner spends cognitive energy navigating an LMS, hunting for a course link, or waiting on an enrollment confirmation, that is attention diverted from actual learning. When an L&D team spends hours formatting reports, sending reminder emails, or updating spreadsheets, that is design capacity that cannot go toward improving the learning experience itself.

**Design response:** Remove it, automate it, or systematize it. This is where AI tools and agentic systems provide the clearest return. An agent that handles enrollment notifications, surfaces the right resource at the right moment, or generates a manager report from completion data is doing exactly what it should -- taking administrative load off humans so humans can focus on what requires human judgment.

**The diagnostic question:** Is this friction produced by the system, or by the subject matter? If it is produced by the system -- by process, logistics, or bureaucracy -- it is administrative friction. Automate it.

---

## Type 2: Content Friction

**Definition:** The inherent complexity of what is being learned. A new concept, a technical skill, an unfamiliar framework. This friction exists because the subject matter is genuinely difficult, not because the design is poor.

Content friction is not the enemy. It is a signal that real learning is being attempted. The design response is not to eliminate it but to scaffold it -- to break it into manageable pieces, sequence it appropriately, provide worked examples, and give learners the support they need to move through complexity without losing the thread.

Eliminating content friction altogether produces the illusion of learning. A learner who navigates a beautifully designed module and answers every question correctly -- because the module essentially answered the questions for them -- has not learned. They have been processed.

**Design response:** Scaffold, sequence, and support. AI tools are useful here when they adapt to where a learner is struggling, surface relevant examples, or provide formative feedback that helps the learner move forward. The key constraint: the learner must still do the cognitive work. AI that completes the thinking for the learner has eliminated content friction, and with it, the learning.

**The diagnostic question:** Is this friction because the subject is hard, or because the design is unclear? If the subject is genuinely complex, scaffold it. If the design is introducing unnecessary complexity, simplify the design -- that is administrative friction wearing a different mask.

---

## Type 3: Growth Friction

**Definition:** The deep thinking, judgment calls, synthesis, and productive struggle that actually build capability. This is the friction that should be protected. It is not a design problem to be solved. It is the point.

Growth friction is what happens when a manager works through a difficult coaching conversation rather than receiving a script. It is what happens when a new employee figures out how to apply a policy to a messy real-world situation rather than being told the answer. It is what happens when a learner makes a mistake, reflects on it, and adjusts. These moments are irreplaceable. No tool, AI or otherwise, can build judgment for someone.

The most serious risk of poorly designed AI in learning systems is the elimination of growth friction at scale. A tool that writes the reflection, completes the scenario, or makes the judgment call for the learner has optimized the experience at the expense of the outcome. The learner feels supported. They have not grown.

**Design response:** Protect it. Keep humans in the struggle. If AI is present, its role is to prompt deeper thinking, not to provide the answer. A well-designed AI learning tool asks better questions. It does not remove the need to think.

**The diagnostic question:** Does this friction require a human to wrestle with it in order to build capability? If yes, it is growth friction. Do not automate it. Design for it.

## Using This Framework as a Diagnostic

When evaluating a learning problem or a proposed AI intervention, apply the framework in sequence:

1. Identify what type of friction is present -- administrative, content, or growth.
2. Match the design response to the friction type.
3. If AI is being proposed, ask which friction it is targeting. If it is targeting administrative friction, proceed. If it is targeting content friction, proceed with constraints (the learner must still do the cognitive work). If it is targeting growth friction, stop and interrogate the proposal carefully.

A single learning experience may contain all three types. A well-designed system handles each one differently -- automating the administrative layer, scaffolding the content layer, and protecting the growth layer so learners can show up fully for the work that only they can do.

## Why This Matters for AI in Learning Design

The current wave of AI tools in L&D has produced tremendous capacity to generate content faster. What it has not produced is a reliable method for determining whether that content should be generated at all, or whether AI belongs in a given learning experience in the first place.

This framework is the upstream diagnostic that answers those questions. It is not a judgment against AI tools -- it is a tool for using them with intention. It answers three questions practitioners rarely slow down to ask: Should AI be part of this learning experience? If yes, why -- what problem is it actually solving? And if yes, how -- in a way that serves learning rather than undermining it? The goal is not to automate learning. It is to automate the friction that gets in the way of learning, so that human connection and real thinking stay at the center.
